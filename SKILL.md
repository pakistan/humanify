# Prose Revision

Run these four passes, in order, on the finished draft. Each pass is a
separate read. Do not merge them into one impressionistic sweep. The
passes work because each asks a narrow, checkable question instead of
"is this good?"

The premise: a first draft is made of the phrasings that came easiest,
and the phrasings that come easiest are the ones that have clustered
together in everyone else's writing. Fluency is the thing under
suspicion. Revision is not polish applied to the draft; it is the
draft's adversary.

## Pass 1: The audit

Split the draft one sentence per line and count. These are comparative
questions with numeric answers, so they can't be flattered:

- Sentence lengths. Look at the spread, not the average. A draft whose
  sentences all run 15–25 words is monotone regardless of content.
- Openings. How many sentences start with the subject? With a
  subordinate clause? With a connective? More than ~70% in any one
  pattern is a finding.
- Verbs. Count forms of "to be" as the main verb. Above roughly a third
  is a finding.
- Subject-to-verb distance. Flag any sentence where an inserted phrase
  holds them more than ~8 words apart.
- Nouns. Count Latinate abstractions (-ion, -ment, -ity) versus names
  of actual things and people.
- Triads and parallels. Mark every "X, Y, and Z" construction and every
  paragraph whose sentences share a template. A parallel that started
  as structure has usually started steering content: the third slot of
  a triad fills itself with whatever is lying around. Break any pattern
  you did not choose on purpose.

Fix what the counts expose before moving on. Vary lengths by breaking
one long sentence and joining two short ones, not by padding.

## Pass 2: The strip test

Delete, without judgment, everything in these categories. Then reread.

- Every sentence-initial connective: however, moreover, additionally,
  furthermore, that said, indeed, of course, ultimately, in fact.
- The opening sentence, if its job is to introduce rather than begin.
  The test: is it already moving, or is it generalizing while clearing
  its throat?
- The final paragraph, if it restates.
- Every sentence whose subject is another sentence: announcing what
  comes next, labeling what just happened, summarizing what the
  paragraph did. ("It's worth noting that," "In other words," "This
  illustrates.")
- Every em dash. Nothing is wrong with the punctuation itself, and this
  is not a rule about grammar. It is a rule about frequency: language
  models reach for the em dash far more often than human writers do, so
  the mark now reads as a signature of machine drafting rather than as a
  choice, and a page of them repeats one interrupted rhythm until the
  reader hears it. Recast with a comma, a colon, a semicolon, or a full
  stop, whichever the sentence actually wants. Where a sentence needs
  the interruption and no other mark supplies it, keep that one.

Restore only what the prose demonstrably fails without. A reversal
usually still reads as a reversal with "however" gone; if it doesn't,
prefer "but," and only then "however." Expect to restore almost
nothing. Whatever stays cut never belonged.

## Pass 3: The volunteer-sentence pass

Read each remaining sentence and ask: is this phrasing the first thing
that would occur to anyone writing about this? Pre-assembled phrases
give themselves away: they arrive whole, they'd fit a hundred other
documents, and they say slightly less than they seem to. ("Plays a
crucial role." "In today's fast-paced world." "A testament to.")

Rewrite flagged sentences from the thought, not from the words. You
cannot fix a sentence using only the words it already contains. If
those were the right words, the sentence wouldn't need fixing. Ask
what the sentence is actually asserting, then say that, plainly, with
the specific detail the stock phrase was standing in for. An
evaluative adjective ("significant," "robust," "challenging") is
usually a specific fact wearing a disguise; write the fact.

While here, check metaphors: a good one is swift, literally accurate,
and expires in a flash. Cut any metaphor extended past one sentence.

## Pass 4: The enactment check

Find every sentence that asserts a quality instead of having it:

- Asserted importance ("This is a crucial point"). Make the sentence
  carrying the point strong enough to feel important, then cut the
  assertion.
- Asserted empathy ("I understand how frustrating this is"). Enact it
  by engaging the specifics of the problem.
- Asserted confidence or borrowed authority ("Studies suggest,"
  "Experts agree"). Where the claim can stand on its own evidence,
  let it; where it can't, name the actual source.
- Asserted conclusions after evidence ("This shows that..."). If the
  evidence was arranged well, the conclusion sentence is redundant; if
  it wasn't, the sentence won't save it. Trust the reader to complete
  the thought. The reader you should write for is intelligent and will
  follow anywhere the prose is clear; serve the literal reader too, but
  only by removing ambiguity, never by over-explaining.

Keep every caveat that changes what the reader should do or believe.
Cut every caveat that exists as insurance.

## When to stop

Revise toward brevity, directness, clarity, rhythm, implication, and
variation. Stop when another pass produces only lateral changes:
different, not better. Done is a compromise, not a state of
perfection; recognize it and release the draft.
